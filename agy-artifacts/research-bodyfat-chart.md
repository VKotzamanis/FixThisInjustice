# Research: licensed visual body-fat-percentage chart for the fitness app

## Bottom line

No properly licensed, redistributable visual asset exists that shows body silhouettes/photos labeled with **body-fat-percentage** numbers, split male/female, ready to drop into the app. The single closest candidate found (Pulvers' silhouette showcard, reproduced in a CC BY PLOS article) is an ordinal 1–9 **body-size/BMI** scale, not a body-fat-percentage scale, and carries a residual chain-of-title caveat because the image itself is attributed to a 2004 non-open journal. See verdict below — marked "usable with caveats," not a clean yes.

## Accuracy evidence: how good is visual/photographic self-estimation of body fat vs. DXA/hydrostatic weighing?

This matters independently of licensing, because it determines whether the app should ship such a chart at all, and if so, how it must be framed.

**Trained-observer studies (not the app's use case, but the ceiling of what's achievable):**
- Sterner TG, Burke EJ (1986), *The Physician and Sportsmedicine* 14(4):101–107, "Body Fat Assessment: A Comparison of Visual Estimation and Skinfold Techniques." 71 male volunteers underwent skinfold measurement + hydrostatic (underwater) weighing and were photographed front/side/rear; two **experienced expert raters** compared the photos to five sets of reference photos. Finding: some experienced raters could visually estimate % fat about as accurately as skinfold-equation prediction — but this required trained, experienced raters, not the subjects rating themselves.
- Eckerson JM, Housh TJ, Johnson GO (1992), PMID 1569858, *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, "The validity of visual estimations of percent body fat in lean males." 35 Caucasian men, mean 9.6 ± 2.3% body fat (narrow, lean range). Two experienced male raters estimated % fat visually. Rater 1: total error 2.3% fat vs. 2.4% fat for skinfield equations (comparable). But mean difference **between the two raters was 2.7% fat** — i.e., even trained raters disagree with each other by an amount comparable to the total error against the criterion method — and the authors' own conclusion was to **recommend skinfold equations over visual inspection** for this reason. Also note the sample was narrow and lean; these numbers do not generalize to overweight/obese bodies, where fat distribution is more heterogeneous and visual cues are less reliable.

**Self-estimation by laypeople (the app's actual use case) — this is the evidence that should drive the product decision:**
- CRONICAS Cohort Study (Peru), PMC6442421 / PMID 30984403: agreement between self-perceived and BIA-measured weight/adiposity status was poor — weighted kappa 0.20–0.31 across settings. **49.9% of participants underestimated** their weight/adiposity status; underestimators had on average **2.4 percentage points more body fat** than they perceived.
- Athletes vs. non-athletes study, PMID 25924565: of overweight/obese participants, **42% underestimated** their own weight status as "normal."
- General pattern across these self-perception studies: overweight/obese individuals — the population that most needs an accurate estimate — are also the population most likely to systematically underestimate their own adiposity from visual/subjective self-assessment.

**Modern computer-vision comparison (informative but not the same task):**
- npj Digital Medicine 2024 (10.1038/s41746-024-01380-6) and 2022 (10.1038/s41746-022-00628-3): AI-driven 2D-photo algorithms (regression on extracted body-shape features, not naked-eye comparison) achieve correlation/CCC ≥ 0.96 with DXA. This confirms that *photographs contain enough signal* to estimate body fat well — but only when processed by a fitted statistical/ML model, not when a person eyeballs a static reference chart. It is not evidence that a printed silhouette chart is accurate for self-assessment.

**Conclusion for the app:** Naked-eye visual self-estimation against a static chart is a fundamentally different, much weaker method than either trained-expert visual rating or AI photo-based estimation, and the direct evidence on lay self-perception shows poor agreement (kappa ~0.2–0.3) with a strong, systematic bias toward underestimating body fat — worst in exactly the overweight/obese users who would most want the tool. If the app ships a visual chart, it should not be presented as a measurement; it needs an explicit accuracy disclaimer (kappa/underestimation figures above) and should point users toward BIA, skinfold, or DXA for anything decision-relevant.
