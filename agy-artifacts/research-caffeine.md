# Caffeine as an Ergogenic Aid for Resistance Training and General Gym Use — Verified Evidence

Prepared 2026-09-04. Every numeric claim below was checked against a source that was actually fetched (WebFetch) or, where fetch was blocked by a paywall/cookie-wall, corroborated across multiple independent WebSearch results and the underlying DOI was confirmed to resolve via the Crossref API. Anything not independently verified is flagged **PARAPHRASE** (someone else's synthesis, not primary text I read myself) or **COULD NOT VERIFY**.

---

## 1. Ergogenic dose range (mg/kg body mass)

**3–6 mg/kg body mass**, taken 30–60 min before exercise, is the range the ISSN position stand identifies as consistently ergogenic.

Verbatim from Guest et al. (2021), *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 18:1, DOI: 10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4 (fetched directly, PMC full text):
> "Caffeine has consistently been shown to improve exercise performance when consumed in doses of 3–6 mg/kg body mass."
> "Minimal effective doses of caffeine currently remain unclear but they may be as low as 2 mg/kg body mass."
> "Very high doses of caffeine (e.g. 9 mg/kg) are associated with a high incidence of side-effects and do not seem to be required to elicit an ergogenic effect."

For **resistance exercise specifically**, a dedicated dose-response meta-analysis found the floor is even lower: Grgic (2022), *Nutrition* 97:111604, DOI: 10.1016/j.nut.2022.111604 (abstract fetched via Semantic Scholar API; DOI/journal/volume independently confirmed via Crossref) pooled 12 studies using doses of **0.9–2 mg/kg** and found significant ergogenic effects on muscular strength, muscular endurance, and mean velocity at these low doses — "even lower than previously suggested," roughly one to two cups of coffee.

**Bottom line for the app:** 3–6 mg/kg is the well-established general range (ISSN); as low as ~1–2 mg/kg is sufficient for many resistance-training outcomes specifically, with diminishing returns and rising side-effect risk above ~9 mg/kg.

## 2. Timing before exercise

The ISSN position stand states (same fetched source):
> "The most commonly used timing of caffeine supplementation is 60 min pre-exercise."

This reflects caffeine's pharmacokinetics — plasma levels rise over roughly the first hour after oral ingestion — which is why a ~60-minute window is the evidence-supported default, not an arbitrary folklore number. I did not independently fetch a primary pharmacokinetic source giving an exact time-to-peak-plasma figure, so I am not stating a precise Tmax number here — **COULD NOT VERIFY** an exact minute-level Tmax beyond the position stand's own "60 min" recommendation.

What is folklore vs. evidence: the specific ritual of "exactly 45 minutes before you touch a barbell" is not supported by any dose-timing RCT I could verify; the evidence supports a broad window (roughly 30–60 min pre-exercise is what capsule-based resistance-training studies most commonly use, per the Grgic dose-response literature discussed in Q1), not a precise optimum minute. Treat "60 min" as a reasonable, evidence-anchored default, not a hard requirement.

## 3. Does the evidence differ for resistance training vs. endurance?

Yes. The ISSN position stand (fetched) says aerobic/endurance exercise shows the most consistent benefit:
> "Aerobic endurance appears to be the form of exercise with the most consistent moderate-to-large benefits" from caffeine, while for strength/power, "small improvements in muscular strength likely have the greatest practical meaningfulness for athletes competing in strength-based sports."

A 2026 narrative review, Domaszewski, *Nutrients* 18(2):328, DOI: 10.3390/nu18020328 (fetched directly via PMC12844960) states this more explicitly:
> "Evidence for ergogenic effects of coffee in resistance exercise and high-intensity tasks is less consistent" compared to endurance exercise, where effects are "broadly comparable to those of isolated caffeine."

So: endurance performance shows the most consistent, largest effects; resistance/strength/power effects are real (see Q1, Grgic 2022) but smaller and more variable across studies.

## 4. Upper safe daily intake (EFSA and FDA)

**EFSA** (Scientific Opinion on the safety of caffeine, *EFSA Journal* 2015;13(5):4102, DOI: 10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102 — DOI existence/metadata confirmed via Crossref API; content read via EFSA's own consumer-facing summary at eufic.org, fetched directly):
- Single dose: **up to 200 mg** is safe for healthy adults.
- Total daily intake: **up to 400 mg/24h** is not likely to cause harm in healthy adults.
- Pregnant women: **up to 200 mg/day** (habitual) does not raise safety concerns for the fetus.
- Children/adolescents: a conservative **3 mg/kg body weight/day**.

**FDA** ("Spilling the Beans: How Much Caffeine is Too Much?", fda.gov, fetched directly):
> "400 milligrams a day — that's about two to three 12-fluid-ounce cups of coffee — as an amount not generally associated with negative effects."

The FDA page also states toxic effects (e.g., seizures) can occur with rapid consumption of roughly **1,200 mg** of caffeine. This is a single-dose acute-toxicity threshold, not an ergogenic-dosing recommendation — do not conflate it with Q1's 3–6 mg/kg.

**Bottom line: EFSA and FDA converge on ≈400 mg/day and ≈200 mg/single dose as the healthy-adult ceiling.** For a ~90 kg lifter, a 6 mg/kg ergogenic dose (540 mg) would already exceed both regulators' single-dose and near the daily guidance — worth flagging in the app's dosing UI for larger athletes.

## 5. Habituation — does habitual coffee drinking blunt the effect?

The evidence is genuinely mixed, and the field's own position stand says so. ISSN position stand (fetched, PMC7777221):
> Research findings remain mixed — one study found tolerance developed after 4 weeks of supplementation, another showed ergogenic effects persisted over 20 days with attenuated magnitude, and "a recent double-blind trial concluded 'no influence of habitual caffeine intake'" on performance improvements.

The most direct dedicated meta-analysis, Carvalho et al. (2022), *Sports Medicine* 52(9):2209–2220, DOI: 10.1007/s40279-022-01685-0 (DOI/citation independently confirmed via Crossref API; I could not get past the Springer paywall/cookie-wall to read the abstract text itself, so its exact effect-size conclusion is **PARAPHRASE**, sourced from secondary descriptions), used three-level meta-analysis/meta-regression across habitual vs. non-habitual, trained vs. untrained users.

A follow-on primary study I did fetch directly, Khodadadi et al. (2025), *Sports Health* 17(5):930–941, DOI: 10.1177/19417381251315093 (fetched via PMC11795567), reports:
> "Habitual caffeine consumption reduced the ergogenic effect of caffeine in both aerobic and anaerobic trials," with the reduction "higher in trained than in untrained volunteers."

**Bottom line:** the honest answer is "probably some attenuation, mechanistically plausible via adenosine-receptor upregulation, but the size of the effect is not settled" — the ISSN position stand explicitly calls the literature mixed, and the most recent primary data I could fetch (Khodadadi 2025) found habituation does reduce the ergogenic effect, more so in trained lifters. I did not independently verify Carvalho et al.'s own numeric effect size (paywalled) — **flag this specific number as PARAPHRASE if quoted downstream.**

## 6. Contraindications / populations who should not follow a standard caffeine recommendation

Primary source, Temple et al. (2017), "The Safety of Ingested Caffeine: A Comprehensive Review," *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 8:80, DOI: 10.3389/fpsyt.2017.00080 (fetched directly, full HTML):

- **Pregnancy:** ACOG recommends limiting caffeine to **<200 mg/day**; EU Scientific Committee/Health Canada put the ceiling at **≤300 mg/day**. Meta-analyses cited show each additional 100 mg/day raises pregnancy-loss risk by ~7%, and intake >700 mg/day raised pregnancy-loss risk by 72%. (Consistent with EFSA's 200 mg/day figure in Q4.)
- **Anxiety disorders:** "Caffeine-induced anxiety disorder" is a recognized DSM diagnosis; anxiety can occur across "a wide range of doses (200–2,000 mg/day)," particularly in psychiatric populations, and higher caffeine use correlates with more anxiety symptoms and relapse risk in bipolar disorder.
- **Adolescents/children:** Body-weight scaling means the same absolute dose (e.g., a 45 mg soda) is a much higher mg/kg dose in a child (2.25 mg/kg in a 20 kg child) than an adult (0.6 mg/kg in a 70 kg adult); caffeine (mainly from soda/energy drinks) is linked to daytime sleepiness, anger, and self-reported violent behavior/conduct disorders in this age group. The American Academy of Pediatrics concludes dietary caffeine "should be discouraged for all children."
- **Cardiac arrhythmia:** Acute caffeine can produce dose-dependent tachycardia/bradycardia and, per this review, may lead to "supraventricular and ventricular tachyarrhythmias, especially at high doses," partly via adenosine-receptor blockade — the same receptor system implicated in caffeine's ergogenic mechanism. A companion source, the Institute of Medicine's *Caffeine in Food and Dietary Supplements: Examining Safety* workshop summary (National Academies Press, 2014; fetched directly via NCBI Bookshelf NBK202224), reports the "prevailing evidence shows no increase in arrhythmia" at typical intakes in the general population, but flags adolescents as a population of concern (citing energy-drink-associated deaths) and notes ≥10 cups coffee/day was associated with a large increase in sudden-cardiac-death odds in one cited study.

**Bottom line for the app:** the standard 3–6 mg/kg recommendation should carry an explicit exclusion/consult-a-doctor flag for pregnancy, diagnosed anxiety or arrhythmia, and users under 18.

## 7. Anhydrous caffeine vs. coffee — is there a difference in ergogenic effect?

For **endurance exercise**, no meaningful difference at matched doses. Hodgson, Randell & Jeukendrup (2013), *PLOS ONE* 8(4):e59561, DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0059561 (fetched directly): crossover trial in 8 trained cyclists/triathletes, 5 mg/kg anhydrous caffeine vs. 5 mg/kg instant coffee vs. decaf vs. placebo, 1 h pre-exercise. Both caffeine and coffee improved a 45-min time trial by ~4.5–4.9% vs. placebo/decaf, "with no significant differences between the caffeine and coffee conditions."

For **resistance/strength and sprint performance**, the picture is less clean. Trexler et al. (2016), *European Journal of Sport Science* 16(6):702–710, DOI: 10.1080/17461391.2015.1085097 (DOI and full citation confirmed via Crossref; full text was paywalled so the result below is **PARAPHRASE** from secondary search summaries, not independently read by me): 54 resistance-trained men, 300 mg anhydrous caffeine vs. an 8.9 g instant-coffee dose matched to 303 mg caffeine vs. placebo, 30 min pre-exercise — neither coffee nor anhydrous caffeine improved 1RM strength more than placebo, though both similarly blunted the drop-off in sprint power across repeated sprints.

The 2026 Domaszewski review (fetched, PMC12844960) argues coffee and isolated caffeine should **not** be treated as interchangeable in general, in part because isolated/anhydrous caffeine "appears to be associated with a higher risk of acute side effects," and coffee's other constituents (chlorogenic acids, etc.) can alter absorption kinetics — but for the specific endurance-exercise comparison above, matched-dose equivalence held.

**Bottom line:** when caffeine content is actually matched, coffee and anhydrous caffeine give equivalent ergogenic benefit for endurance; for resistance/sprint performance the matched-dose comparative evidence is thinner and doesn't show anhydrous caffeine to be reliably superior to equal-dose coffee.

## 8. Milk and sugar in pre-workout coffee — any evidence they blunt the ergogenic effect?

**No evidence found — this is folklore, not a supported claim.** I searched specifically for studies isolating milk, cream, or sugar/carbohydrate additives in a pre-workout coffee and their effect on caffeine's ergogenic action. The most relevant fetched source, the Domaszewski 2026 Nutrients review (DOI: 10.3390/nu18020328, PMC12844960), explicitly does not isolate this variable — it only notes coffee "may be co-ingested with carbohydrates, milk, or other nutrients" and that such factors "can influence gastric emptying, absorption kinetics, and subjective tolerance" as a general pharmacokinetic point, and separately notes co-ingested carbohydrate may aid post-exercise glycogen resynthesis (a different, unrelated benefit, not evidence of blunting). No fetched or searched source reported a controlled comparison of black coffee vs. milk-and-sugar coffee on an ergogenic outcome.

**Bottom line: state plainly to users that "milk or sugar in your pre-workout coffee ruins the effect" is not supported by any study I could find — mark it explicitly as unverified folklore in the app copy, not as a caveat with real evidence behind it.**
