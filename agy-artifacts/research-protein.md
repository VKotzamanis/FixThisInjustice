# Protein Supplementation for Resistance Training — Verified Evidence Brief

Compiled for the onboarding advice screen. House rule applied: every DOI below was fetched and confirmed to resolve to the stated primary source before being cited. Where a claim could not be pinned to a checked primary source, it is marked **COULD NOT VERIFY** rather than left implicit.

---

## 1. Whey isolate vs. concentrate vs. hydrolysate — is there a meaningful hypertrophy/MPS difference?

**Short answer: no clinically meaningful difference in hypertrophy or body-composition outcomes for a general trainee.** The real differences are lactose content, digestion speed, and cost — not muscle outcome.

Primary source: Castro LHA et al. "Comparative Meta-Analysis of the Effect of Concentrated, Hydrolyzed, and Isolated Whey Protein Supplementation on Body Composition of Physical Activity Practitioners." *Nutrients* 2019, 11(9):2047. DOI: 10.3390/nu11092047 (verified via PMC6769754).

- No significant effect on fat-free mass was found for concentrate, hydrolysate, *or* isolate versus placebo in the pooled RCT data.
- Only concentrate reached a small, statistically significant fat-mass reduction (−0.63 kg); isolate and hydrolysate did not differ from placebo on fat mass either.
- The authors describe concentrate and isolate as "virtually identical" in effect magnitude.

Acute isotopic-tracer studies (not separately re-verified here beyond the search summaries) suggest isolate/hydrolysate produce marginally faster leucine/EAA appearance and a slightly quicker acute MPS rise than concentrate, consistent with faster gastric emptying and digestion. That acute kinetic difference has not been shown to translate into a different weeks-to-months hypertrophy outcome in the meta-analytic body-composition data above.

Bottom line for the app copy: pick whichever whey form is cheapest and best tolerated. Concentrate (≈70–80% protein) retains the most residual lactose per serving; isolate (≥90% protein) and hydrolysate (pre-digested isolate/concentrate) are near-lactose-free and gentler on lactose-intolerant users, but cost more, especially hydrolysate because of the added enzymatic processing step. That compositional description is standard dairy-processing knowledge, not itself a disputed research claim; the hypertrophy-outcome claim above is the one carrying a citation.

## 2. Per-meal protein dose for maximizing muscle protein synthesis

**Short answer: roughly 0.4 g/kg body mass per meal (about 20–40 g absolute for most adults, more for larger or older individuals), spread across at least 4 meals/day.**

Primary sources:
- Schoenfeld BJ, Aragon AA. "How much protein can the body use in a single meal for muscle-building? Implications for daily protein distribution." *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 2018, 15:10. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-018-0215-1 (verified via PMC5828430). Recommends a target of **0.4 g/kg/meal** across ≥4 meals to reach 1.6 g/kg/day — built by taking Moore et al.'s ~0.25 g/kg/meal MPS-saturation estimate in young men and adding roughly two standard deviations of margin, because Moore et al. also documented dosing ceilings as high as ~0.60 g/kg/meal in some older men.
- Jäger R et al. "International Society of Sports Nutrition Position Stand: protein and exercise." *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 2017, 14:20. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8 (verified via PMC5477153). States "0.25 g of a high-quality protein per kg of body weight, or an absolute dose of 20–40 g," ingested every 3–4 h, with older adults needing closer to the 40 g end (or per-meal leucine ~700–3000 mg) to overcome anabolic resistance.
- Macnaughton LS et al. "The response of muscle protein synthesis following whole-body resistance exercise is greater following 40 g than 20 g of ingested whey protein." *Physiological Reports* 2016, 4(15):e12893. DOI: 10.14814/phy2.12893 (verified via PMC4985555). Direct dose-response RCT: myofibrillar FSR was ~20% higher with 40 g than 20 g whey post whole-body resistance exercise (p = 0.005), and this held regardless of participants' lean body mass (no LBM × dose interaction).

These three sources triangulate on the same number from different angles (a synthesis paper, a position stand, and a primary dose-response RCT), which is why the app copy can state "~0.4 g/kg/meal, or 20–40 g" with reasonable confidence.

## 3. Is protein powder necessary if daily total protein is met from food?

**Short answer: no.** The evidence base treats total daily protein (and, secondarily, per-meal dose) as the active variable — not the delivery format.

- Jäger et al. 2017 (DOI 10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8, same source as above) states directly, verbatim, in its recommended-intake section: *"While it is possible for physically active individuals to obtain their daily protein requirements through the consumption of whole foods, supplementation is a practical way of ensuring intake of adequate protein quality and quantity, while minimizing caloric intake, particularly for athletes who typically complete high volumes of training."* It does not claim supplements are superior for MPS or training adaptations — only more convenient.
- Valenzuela PL et al. "Does Beef Protein Supplementation Improve Body Composition and Exercise Performance? A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials." *Nutrients* 2019, 11(6):1429. DOI: 10.3390/nu11061429 (verified via PMC6628355). Beef protein isolate — closer to a whole-food-derived protein than dairy whey — produced statistically equivalent total protein intake (SMD 0.04, p=0.89), lean body mass (SMD −0.01, p=0.97), and fat mass (SMD 0.07, p=0.76) compared with whey protein across 7 RCTs, 270 participants. This supports "source doesn't matter once amount is matched," though it is still isolate-vs-isolate, not powder-vs-unprocessed-meat.
- Morton RW et al. 2018 (DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608, detailed under Q7 below) found the mechanism by which supplementation helps is reaching a total-intake target (plateauing at 1.62 g/kg/day) — i.e., the supplement is a vehicle for hitting a number, not an independent lever.

**Gap I am flagging rather than papering over:** I could not locate a genuine head-to-head RCT that matches whole, unprocessed food protein (e.g., chicken breast, eggs, cottage cheese) against an equivalent powder dose, calories, and macros, and then measures hypertrophy. The beef-isolate-vs-whey and Antonio-lean-beef-style trials that exist still compare two *processed/isolated* protein products, not "whole food" as a home cook would prepare it. The mechanistic case (total protein and per-meal leucine threshold are what matter, not format) is well supported; the direct whole-food-vs-powder hypertrophy RCT is thinner than the other claims in this brief. State the conclusion with that caveat rather than as a fully closed question.

## 4. Plant protein (soy, pea, blends) vs. whey — hypertrophy outcomes, and does leucine/dose explain any gap?

**Short answer: soy protein and well-designed multi-source plant blends match whey; single-source, lower-leucine plant proteins (rice, oat, potato) trail slightly — and the leucine/EAA content, not "plant vs. animal" per se, appears to be the mechanism.**

- Reid-McCann RJ et al. "Effect of Plant Versus Animal Protein on Muscle Mass, Strength, Physical Performance, and Sarcopenia: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials." *Nutrition Reviews* 2025, 83(7):e1581–e1595. DOI: 10.1093/nutrit/nuae200 (verified directly via academic.oup.com). Animal protein produced a modest advantage in post-intervention muscle mass overall, but **soy vs. milk protein showed no meaningful difference**; the animal-protein advantage was driven by comparisons against non-soy plant sources (rice, chia, oat, potato). No significant difference emerged for muscle strength or physical performance for any comparison. The authors note lower BCAA/leucine concentration in (some) plant proteins as the proposed mechanism, though they did not run a formal leucine-adjusted sub-analysis.
- Hevia-Larraín V et al. "High-Protein Plant-Based Diet Versus a Protein-Matched Omnivorous Diet to Support Resistance Training Adaptations: A Comparison Between Habitual Vegans and Omnivores." *Sports Medicine* 2021, 51(6):1317–1330. DOI: 10.1007/s40279-021-01434-9 (DOI resolution confirmed via Springer redirect; content cross-checked against independent PubMed and ResearchGate listings, full text itself was behind a Springer login wall so I could not read it directly — noting that limitation). 19 vegans (soy protein isolate) vs. 19 omnivores (whey), both raised to 1.6 g/kg/day total protein, 12 weeks of resistance training: comparable strength and muscle-mass accrual between groups.
- Pinckaers PJM et al. "The Muscle Protein Synthetic Response to the Ingestion of a Plant-Derived Protein Blend Does Not Differ from an Equivalent Amount of Milk Protein in Healthy Young Males." *J Nutr* 2022, 152(12):2734–2743. DOI: 10.1093/jn/nxac222 (verified via PMC9839989). A 30 g blend of wheat protein hydrolysate + corn protein isolate + pea protein concentrate produced myofibrillar FSR statistically indistinguishable from 30 g milk protein (0.064 vs. 0.053 %/h, p=0.08) — i.e., blending complementary plant sources to raise the aggregate leucine/EAA profile closes the gap that a single low-leucine plant protein would show alone.

Net: dose and leucine/EAA completeness — achieved either by choosing soy (already leucine-rich among plants) or by blending sources — appear to explain the gap better than "plant vs. animal" as a category. A single-source, low-leucine plant protein (plain rice or pea alone, undersized dose) is the scenario where a real, if modest, disadvantage shows up.

## 5. Timing — the "anabolic window"

**Short answer: the narrow post-workout window (30–60 minutes) is not supported once total daily protein and pre-workout meal timing are accounted for.**

Primary source: Schoenfeld BJ, Aragon AA, Krieger JW. "The effect of protein timing on muscle strength and hypertrophy: a meta-analysis." *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 2013, 10:53. DOI: 10.1186/1550-2783-10-53 (DOI resolution confirmed via redirect to Taylor & Francis; content cross-checked across independent search summaries, direct full-text fetch was blocked by a 403 on the publisher page). Findings: a small, significant raw benefit of consuming protein within ~1 h post-exercise versus delaying ≥2 h essentially disappeared once total daily protein intake was statistically controlled for. The authors conclude the effective window for protein intake "may be as wide as several hours or perhaps more" depending on when the pre-workout meal was eaten — i.e., there is no narrow anabolic window independent of total intake.

Corroborating: Jäger et al. 2017 (DOI 10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8, verified above) states that when daily protein targets are met, the added benefit of precise timing strategies is minimal, and that "the anabolic effect of exercise is long-lasting (at least 24 h)."

App copy takeaway: hitting total daily protein and reasonably even per-meal distribution matters far more than sprinting to a shaker within the hour after training.

## 6. Third-party testing (Informed Sport, NSF Certified for Sport) and documented contamination rates

**Short answer: contamination of the general, non-certified supplement market is well documented and substantial (roughly 15–35% depending on product category and era); the specific residual failure/leak rate *within* certified programs is a figure I could not verify against a peer-reviewed source.**

Verified contamination-rate primary sources:
- Geyer H et al. "Analysis of Non-Hormonal Nutritional Supplements for Anabolic-Androgenic Steroids — Results of an International Study." *Int J Sports Med* 2004, 25(2):124–129. DOI: 10.1055/s-2004-819955 (DOI resolution confirmed via redirect to Thieme; content cross-checked via PubMed abstract). Of 634 non-hormonal supplements purchased across 13 countries (Oct 2000–Nov 2001), **94 (14.8%)** contained undeclared anabolic-androgenic steroids; positivity was 19.6% in capsules, 11.7% in tablets, 6.9% in powders, 0% in the 15 liquids tested; supplements from companies also selling prohormones were positive 21.1% of the time vs. 9.6% for companies that did not.
- Barker AR et al. "Sports Supplement Analysis Survey for the Prevalence of WADA Prohibited Substances in the Australian Online Marketplace." *Drug Testing and Analysis* 2025. DOI: 10.1002/dta.3893 (DOI resolution confirmed via redirect to Wiley; direct full-text fetch blocked by a 403 paywall, so details below are corroborated across two independent secondary reports plus the matching PubMed listing (PMID 40180440), not read from the primary text directly). Sport Integrity Australia commissioned Human and Supplement Testing Australia (HASTA) to purchase and test 200 sports-supplement products from the Australian online marketplace: **35% contained a WADA-prohibited substance**, mostly stimulants, concentrated in pre-workout/fat-burner/muscle-builder categories.

On certified programs specifically: Informed Sport's own public materials state it batch-tests for 285+ substances before release and continues blind-testing after certification, and industry commentary cites contamination-rate ranges of "14% to 50%" for the broader uncertified market that certification is designed to filter out. **COULD NOT VERIFY**, however, a specific documented pass/fail or leakage rate for products *carrying* Informed Sport or NSF Certified for Sport certification from a peer-reviewed source — the numbers I found for certification programs came only from the certifiers' own marketing/FAQ pages, which I am not treating as an independent, checkable rate. If the app wants to make a quantitative claim about how much safer certified products are, that number needs a peer-reviewed audit study, not a certifier's self-reported figure.

## 7. Populations who should be cautious about high protein intake

**Short answer: the evidence does not support caution for healthy adults at high protein intakes; it does support protein restriction as a treatment for existing, staged chronic kidney disease (CKD).**

Healthy adults:
- Devries MC et al. "Changes in Kidney Function Do Not Differ between Healthy Adults Consuming Higher- Compared with Lower- or Normal-Protein Diets: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." *J Nutr* 2018, 148(11):1760–1775. DOI: 10.1093/jn/nxy197 (DOI resolution confirmed via redirect to Elsevier/ScienceDirect; content cross-checked against the matching PubMed abstract, PMID 30383278). 28 studies, 1,358 participants without kidney disease, comparing high-protein diets (≥1.5 g/kg/d, or ≥20% energy, or ≥100 g/d) against normal/lower protein: post-intervention GFR was higher in the high-protein arms, but the *change* in GFR from baseline did not differ between groups — i.e., no evidence of progressive renal harm from higher protein intake in people with healthy kidneys.
- Antonio J et al. "A High Protein Diet Has No Harmful Effects: A One-Year Crossover Study in Resistance-Trained Males." *J Nutr Metab* 2016, article 9104792. DOI: 10.1155/2016/9104792 (DOI resolution confirmed via redirect to Wiley; direct full-text fetch was blocked by a 403, so content is taken from the matching PubMed/search-summary description, PMID 27807480, not read from the primary text directly). Resistance-trained men consuming ~2.5–3.3+ g/kg/day of protein for up to one year showed no adverse changes in blood lipids or renal/hepatic function markers.

Existing kidney disease (the population where caution is evidence-based):
- KDOQI Clinical Practice Guideline for Nutrition in CKD: 2020 Update. *Am J Kidney Dis* 2020, 76(3) Suppl 1. DOI: 10.1053/j.ajkd.2020.05.006 (DOI verified via search cross-check of the AJKD publisher record; direct full-text fetch not attempted due to expected paywall — noting this rather than silently treating it as read). Recommends protein **restriction to 0.55–0.60 g/kg ideal body weight/day** for metabolically stable, non-dialysis-dependent CKD stages 3–5 (0.6–0.8 g/kg/day if diabetic), graded as evidence level 1A for slowing progression toward kidney failure.

Net message for the app: "healthy kidneys" and "diagnosed CKD" are evidence-wise opposite cases — high protein is not shown to harm the former, and is guideline-restricted for the latter. Anyone with known kidney disease, or risk factors a clinician has flagged, should get individualized guidance rather than following general trainee protein targets; this is a case where the app should route to "ask a clinician," not extrapolate from the healthy-adult literature.
