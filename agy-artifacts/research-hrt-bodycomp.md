## Q1. Lean mass / fat mass changes during gender-affirming hormone therapy (GAHT)

**Magnitudes (verified against primary abstracts):**

- **Klaver et al. 2017** (meta-analysis, 10 studies, 171 transwomen / 354 transmen), *Andrologia* 49(5):e12660, DOI 10.1111/and.12660, PMID 27572683:
  - Transwomen (feminizing HT): body weight +1.8 kg; **fat mass +3.0 kg (95% CI 2.0–3.9)**; **lean body mass −2.4 kg**.
  - Transmen (masculinizing HT): body weight +1.7 kg; **fat mass −2.6 kg (95% CI −3.9 to −1.4)**; **lean body mass +3.9 kg (95% CI 3.2–4.5)**.
  - **Timepoint caveat**: the abstract does not give a single pooled timepoint — it synthesizes studies with heterogeneous follow-up durations. Treat this as "typical change observed across the published follow-up range," not a single time-stamped effect size.

- **Van Caenegem et al. 2012** (cross-sectional), *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 97(7):2503–2511, DOI 10.1210/jc.2012-1187, PMID 22564669. 50 transmen post-surgery + 16 pre-treatment, vs. 66 matched female controls. After testosterone therapy described only as "long-term" (**exact mean duration in years: COULD NOT VERIFY from the abstract text retrieved** — would require the full-text Methods section): higher lean/muscle mass, greater grip strength, lower fat mass than female controls; also larger cortical bone size, lower cortical volumetric BMD at some sites.

- **Van Caenegem et al. 2015** (ENIGI cohort, prospective case-controlled), *Eur J Endocrinol* 172(2):163–171, DOI 10.1530/EJE-14-0586, PMID 25550352. 23 transmen vs. 23 matched control women, **1-year (12-month)** testosterone treatment: **muscle mass +10.4%**, **fat mass −9.7%**, increased bone turnover markers, minor increases in trabecular density (anabolic signal). This is the strongest single-cohort evidence for a defined 12-month timepoint.

- **Fighera et al. 2018**, *Clin Endocrinol (Oxf)* 88(6):856–862, DOI 10.1111/cen.13607, PMID 29630732. 142 transwomen, **mean follow-up 31.3 months (~2.6 years)**: significant increase in total fat mass, significant decrease in appendicular lean mass (ALM); bone mineral density remained stable despite the composition shift. 18% had low bone mass at baseline vs. 0% in reference groups; baseline ALM and fat mass explained 14.9% (lumbar spine) and 20.6% (femur) of BMD variance.

- **Auer et al. 2018**, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 103(2):790–802, DOI 10.1210/jc.2017-01559, PMID 29216353 (title/DOI cross-checked via esummary). 45 transwomen + 24 transmen, ENIGI cohort, **12 months**: transwomen — fat mass increased (P<0.001), lean mass "remained unchanged" (contrasts with Klaver's pooled −2.4 kg — direct disagreement between studies on whether lean mass falls or holds in transwomen at ~1 year, worth flagging as inconsistency rather than smoothing over it); transmen — fat mass decreased (P=0.048), lean mass increased (P<0.001).

**Time course beyond 12 months, from the individual-variation and systematic-review literature:**

- **van Velzen et al. 2020**, *Eur J Endocrinol* 183:529–536, DOI 10.1530/EJE-20-0609, PMID 33071222. 323 transmen / 288 transwomen, followed to **24 months**. Critical finding: **a lack of measurable body-composition change at 24 months was observed in 20.2% of transmen (19/94) and 9.4% of transwomen (9/96)** — i.e., roughly 1 in 5 transmen and 1 in 10 transwomen show no meaningful shift even after two years. Lower testosterone / weaker LH suppression correlated with non-response in transmen; no comparable predictor was found in transwomen. The authors' conclusion is explicitly that individualized, not standardized, timelines are needed.

- **Harper et al. 2021** (systematic review, 24 studies), *Br J Sports Med* 55:865–872, DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2020-103106, PMID **COULD NOT VERIFY exact PMID in this session** (DOI confirmed via CrossRef; PMID not independently re-checked via eutils). Key quoted findings: hemoglobin/hematocrit reach cisgender-female range by ~4 months in transwomen; but **strength, lean body mass, and muscle area in transwomen remain above cisgender-female values even after 36 months** of hormone therapy — i.e., full convergence toward the "female" body-composition phenotype is not complete even at 3 years in this review's synthesis.

**What this means for the app's RMR framing (a reasoning note, not a new citation):** the magnitude and completeness of lean/fat mass change is (a) direction-dependent (transmen converge faster/more completely toward male-typical composition than transwomen converge toward female-typical composition, per Harper 2021), (b) individually variable enough that 1-in-5 to 1-in-10 users may show negligible change even at 24 months (van Velzen 2020), and (c) not fully converged even in long-term users on at least one contested metric (Auer's "unchanged" lean mass in transwomen at 12 months vs. Klaver's pooled −2.4 kg). None of the four "key studies" named in the task measured RMR directly — all measured DXA/bioimpedance body composition, not indirect calorimetry.

## Q2. Has any predictive RMR equation (Mifflin-St Jeor, Harris-Benedict, Cunningham) been validated in transgender populations on HT?

**No. I could not find any published validation study, and this absence held up across multiple independent search strategies** (PubMed-style term combinations for "resting metabolic rate"/"basal metabolic rate" × "transgender"/"transsexual" × "predictive equation"/"indirect calorimetry"/"validation"; general web search; and reading two review articles that would have been the obvious place to cite one if it existed).

Supporting evidence for the gap, all independently checked:

- **Tosi et al. 2026**, *Nutrients* 18(12):1967, DOI 10.3390/nu18121967, PMID 42356353 (title, journal, and DOI confirmed via CrossRef and eutils). This is a 2026 narrative review specifically on nutritional monitoring during GAHT. Its abstract states verbatim: *"current assessment models, largely derived from cisgender populations, may not fully capture hormone-related body composition and metabolic changes."* I searched the retrievable content for "Mifflin," "Harris-Benedict," and "Cunningham" by name — **no mentions were found** in what I could access (full MDPI HTML was blocked by a 403/DNS issue in this session; I relied on the PubMed abstract and a WebFetch summary, not the complete full text — so I cannot rule out that the full text names a specific equation. This is a genuine access gap, not a confirmed absence of that specific discussion).
- **Gold, Perez de Bronner & Goday 2024** (published online 2023), *Nutrition in Clinical Practice* 39(2):366–372, DOI 10.1002/ncp.11049, PMID 37528505. Abstract states verbatim: *"there are few definitive guidelines on providing medical nutrition care to this population,"* and that *"many standardized guidelines and assessment tools are sex-specific,"* creating a stated problem for this population — but the retrievable abstract does not report a validation study either; it is a narrative/perspective piece, not primary data.
- **ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06733415**, "Changes in Energy Expenditure (EE) in Transsexuals Undergoing Hormonal Therapy," sponsor Tel-Aviv Sourasky Medical Center, status "Enrolling by invitation" as of the record fetched, start date 2024-01-03, **no results posted**. This trial explicitly measures resting energy expenditure by (presumably) indirect calorimetry before and 6–12 months after starting HT in ~40 participants — it is the closest thing to a relevant study I found, and it does not yet have data. Its existence is itself evidence that this validation gap is recognized and only now being addressed empirically, not evidence that an answer exists.

**Bottom line for Q2: this is a critical, confirmed absence.** The app should not present Mifflin-St Jeor, Harris-Benedict, or Cunningham as validated for transgender users on GAHT — none of the three has been tested against measured (indirect-calorimetry) RMR in that population in any study I could locate.

## Q3. Does evidence support a specific time-on-HRT threshold (e.g. 6 or 12 months) for switching which sex coefficient fits better?

**No evidence supports a threshold, and this follows necessarily from Q2**: since no study has ever regressed measured RMR against a predictive equation in a transgender cohort at any single timepoint, no study could have compared that fit across timepoints to find a crossover point. I searched directly for this comparison (duration-of-HT × body-composition-change × "threshold"/"plateau") and found:

- **van Velzen et al. 2020** (cited above) is the closest relevant study — it establishes that the *rate* of body-composition change varies enormously between individuals and that a meaningful fraction show no change by 24 months — but it studied DXA-measured lean/fat mass, not RMR or equation fit, and its explicit conclusion argues *against* a fixed timeline of any kind, recommending individualized assessment instead.
- No study I found tests whether an RMR equation's "male" or "female" coefficient describes a transgender person's measured RMR better at any given month of therapy.

**Bottom line for Q3: no evidence supports a threshold at 6, 12, or any other month count. Any such threshold the app might otherwise be tempted to hard-code would be an invented number, not a literature-derived one.**

- **Hembree et al. 2017, Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline**, "Endocrine Treatment of Gender-Dysphoric/Gender-Incongruent Persons," *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 102(11):3869–3903, DOI 10.1210/jc.2017-01658, PMID 28945902 (DOI confirmed via CrossRef, abstract confirmed via eutils). The correct guideline DOI, 10.1210/jc.2017-01658, was confirmed independently via both eutils and CrossRef before use here.)

- **ACSM**: I found no ACSM position stand specifically on RMR/energy-requirement estimation for transgender people. The closest document is **Marra et al. 2026**, "Medical Care of Transgender Athletes and Policy Implications: ACSM Contemporary Issue Statement," *Transl J Am Coll Sports Med* 11(2):e000336, DOI 10.1249/TJX.0000000000000336 (confirmed via CrossRef; no PMID found in this session — possibly not yet indexed in PubMed as of the 2026-04 publication). **Bottom line for Q4: none of the three major professional-body documents I checked (WPATH SOC8, Endocrine Society 2017 CPG, ACSM 2026 transgender-athlete statement) provides guidance on estimating energy requirements or resting metabolic rate for transgender people.** The app cannot cite any of them for RMR-equation guidance because none exists.

## Q5. Is Cunningham (fat-free-mass-based, no sex term) reasoned to be more appropriate than a sex-term equation for this population?

**This is reasoning, not a cited finding — no study I located tests this comparison directly in a transgender cohort (consistent with the Q2 gap).** The mechanistic argument, laid out explicitly so it can be checked rather than taken on authority:

1. Mifflin-St Jeor's sex term is a fixed offset (+5 men / −161 women, a 166 kcal/day gap) that functions as a **proxy for a body-composition difference the equation cannot see directly** — it does not measure fat-free mass, so it uses a self-reported sex flag as a stand-in for "typically more/less lean mass at a given weight/height/age." In a cisgender reference population, that proxy correlates with the higher average lean mass in the "male" category.
2. In a person undergoing GAHT, that proxy is actively being made incorrect. Per Q1, feminizing HT reduces lean mass and raises fat mass over months–years (Klaver: −2.4 kg lean, though Auer's 12-month data disagree and found no significant lean-mass change — a genuine unresolved tension), while masculinizing HT does the reverse (Klaver: +3.9 kg lean; Van Caenegem 2015: +10.4% muscle mass at 12 months). A birth-sex-keyed coefficient reflects the pre-treatment body composition proxy, and a lived-gender-keyed coefficient assumes a fully converged endpoint that (per Harper 2021 and van Velzen 2020) many users have not reached, and some may never fully reach.
3. Cunningham's equation (RMR = 370 + 21.6 × FFM(kg)) uses **the mechanistic variable itself** — measured or estimated fat-free mass — rather than a sex-based proxy for it. If FFM is estimated well (by bioimpedance, skinfolds, or DXA), the equation is, in principle, insensitive to whichever proxy category a Mifflin-St Jeor-style equation would otherwise need, because it does not need a proxy at all.
4. **The caveat that matters**: Cunningham's own derivation (Cunningham JJ, "Body composition and resting metabolic rate: the myth of feminine metabolism," *Am J Clin Nutr* 1991 — **DOI/PMID: COULD NOT VERIFY in this session**; I found the title, journal, year, and the equation itself corroborated by multiple secondary sources, but did not independently confirm the DOI/PMID resolve, so do not cite this as a checked primary source without further verification) was built and validated in a cisgender reference sample. "No sex term" is not the same claim as "validated in transgender bodies" — it removes one specific failure mode (a stale sex-based proxy) without being tested against measured RMR in this population at all. This is the same gap identified in Q2, and it applies to Cunningham exactly as much as to Mifflin-St Jeor.

**Bottom line for Q5: the FFM-based structure is mechanistically more defensible for a population whose body composition is actively diverging from its birth-sex baseline, provided FFM is estimated accurately — but this is an engineering inference from what the equations measure, not a literature finding, and Cunningham is equally unvalidated in this population by direct evidence.**

## Q6. Most respectful and accurate terminology used in the actual literature

Reporting only terms I saw used in the sources fetched in this session, not an invented or house-style term:

- **Older primary studies (2012–2018)** — Van Caenegem, Klaver, Fighera, Auer — consistently use **"transsexual persons"**, **"male-to-female" / "female-to-male"**, and **"cross-sex hormone therapy (CSHT)"**. This is the terminology of the source literature itself, not a term I am choosing to prefer; it reads as dated next to current usage.
- **Current clinical/professional literature (2017–2026)** — Hembree et al. 2017 (Endocrine Society), WPATH SOC8 (2022), Gold et al. 2023/2024, Tosi et al. 2026 — uses **"gender-affirming hormone therapy (GAHT)"**, **"transgender and gender-diverse (TGD) individuals"**, and **"gender-dysphoric/gender-incongruent persons"** (specifically in the Endocrine Society guideline's title).
- **"Sex assigned at birth"** appears as the AMA-recommended term per secondary sources (AMA inclusive-language guidance, described in an AMA PDF I could not extract verbatim text from in this session — **quote-level verification COULD NOT VERIFY**, only the existence and general content of that guidance is attested by secondary description) and is used descriptively (not as a direct quote I verified) in transgender-health literature reviews.
- **"AMAB" / "AFAB"** ("assigned male/female at birth") appear as shorthand in some transgender-health literature per search results, but I did not confirm their use inside any of the primary peer-reviewed papers cited above (they are more common in clinical/community guidance documents than in the endocrinology papers I fetched).
- **"Cisgender"** is the standard paired term for non-transgender people, used by APA and AMA guidance per secondary sources.

**Recommendation for the app, stated as a design inference, not a citation**: use **"sex assigned at birth"** for the input field the RMR equation needs, and **"gender-affirming hormone therapy"** for the treatment variable — these are the terms the current (post-2017) clinical and professional literature actually uses, as opposed to the "transsexual"/"cross-sex hormone therapy" phrasing found only in the older primary studies that happen to carry the numeric evidence in Q1.
