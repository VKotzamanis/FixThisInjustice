# Content peer review — FixThisInjustice training app

**Date:** 2026-09-01
**Reviewer scope:** scientific content only. Code structure, style, architecture and UI are another reviewer's remit and are not assessed here.
**Files reviewed:** `data.js` (288 lines, full), `console-content.js` (711 lines, full), and the numeric rules only in `console-store.jsx` (404 lines) and `console-train.jsx` (443 lines).
**Purpose:** the app encodes one named individual's 24-week plan and is being generalised to a group. This review separates what is defensible for anyone, what is defensible only for that individual, and what is not defensible at all.

## Method

Every DOI cited below was resolved against `https://api.crossref.org/works/<DOI>` and the returned title recorded. Where a claim rested on a paywalled full text, that is stated in the row. Six verification agents ran in parallel; 415 source fetches are logged in `REFERENCES.md` under "Content peer-review sources", including failed fetches, so each claim of absence is auditable rather than asserted. Two DOIs in common circulation were confirmed **not to resolve** (HTTP 404): `10.1080/17461391.2017.1372855` (Grgic 2017 — correct is `10.1080/17461391.2017.1340524`) and `10.2165/00007256-200939090-00003` (de Salles 2009 — correct is `10.2165/11315230-000000000-00000`).

Arithmetic and internal-consistency checks were computed directly against the source files, not asserted. Where a number is my own arithmetic rather than a published figure, the row says so.

## 5. The 42 specimen cards

Every `source` string was checked for (a) whether the cited work exists and (b) whether it supports the card's specific claim. **Tier totals: common 0 SUPPORTED / 8 PARTIALLY / 7 WRONG; uncommon 2 / 7 / 6; rare 1 SUPPORTED / 2 PARTIALLY / 1 UNSUPPORTED / 8 WRONG.**

Three failure patterns recur and matter more than any single card:

1. **Wrong journal attached to the right paper** — c002, c004, c008, c013, r007, u007, and a wrong year on c014 and u009. # Deliverable 2 — Generic-user engine: recommended equations

**The blocking problem.** The setup screen (`console-shared.jsx`, `Setup`) collects **one field: the programme start date.** Grep confirms the strings `age`, `height`, `sex`, `female`, `male` appear nowhere in `data.js`, `console-store.jsx` or `console-train.jsx` except as SVG attributes and card prose. Each equation below therefore ends with the parameters setup must collect, sex-specific ones flagged.

**One cross-cutting finding first.** Of everything below, **only fluid intake takes a sex term.** Roberts BM, Nuckols G, Krieger JW (2020), *Sex Differences in Resistance Training: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis*, J Strength Cond Res 34(5):1448-1460, DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000003521, found **no** sex difference in hypertrophy (ES=0.07±0.06, P=0.31, I²=0) or lower-body strength (ES=−0.21±0.16, P=0.20), and an effect **favouring females** for relative upper-body strength (ES=−0.60±0.16, P=0.002). ## 1. Resting metabolic rate

**Default when body fat is UNKNOWN — Mifflin-St Jeor.**
```
Male:   RMR = 10·weight(kg) + 6.25·height(cm) − 5·age(y) + 5
Female: RMR = 10·weight(kg) + 6.25·height(cm) − 5·age(y) − 161
```
Mifflin MD, St Jeor ST, Hill LA, Scott BJ, Daugherty SA, Koh YO (1990). *A new predictive equation for resting energy expenditure in healthy individuals*. Am J Clin Nutr 51(2):241-247. DOI 10.1093/ajcn/51.2.241 — **verified via Crossref, exact title.** n=498 (251 M / 247 F), 19-78 y, indirect calorimetry.

**Why it wins.** Frankenfield DC (2013), *Bias and accuracy of resting metabolic rate equations in non-obese and obese adults*, Clin Nutr 32(6):976-982, DOI 10.1016/j.clnu.2013.03.022 (verified), n=337: Mifflin-St Jeor accurate in **82% overall (87% non-obese, 75% obese)**, and **unbiased** (95% CI −26 to +8 kcal/d) where all other equations tended to overestimate. Frankenfield D, Roth-Yousey L, Compher C (2005), J Am Diet Assoc 105(5):775-789, DOI 10.1016/j.jada.2005.02.005 (verified), concludes verbatim that it is "the most reliable, predicting RMR within 10% of measured in more nonobese and obese individuals than any other equation."

> **Caveat carried forward:** the widely-quoted "82% non-obese / 70% obese" pair attributed to the **2005** paper is **COULD NOT VERIFY** (paywalled; the abstract contains no percentages). Use the 2013 figures above, which were verified.

**Default when body fat IS known — Cunningham (1991), FFM-based.**
```
RMR = 370 + 21.6 · FFM(kg)
```
Cunningham JJ (1991). *Body composition as a determinant of energy expenditure: a synthetic review and a proposed general prediction equation*. Am J Clin Nutr 54(6):963-969. DOI 10.1093/ajcn/54.6.963 — **verified.** Explains 65-90% of REE variation. The earlier Cunningham JJ (1980), Am J Clin Nutr 33(11):2372-2374, DOI 10.1093/ajcn/33.11.2372 (verified), gives `BMR = 500 + 22·LBM` — a genuinely different equation from a reanalysis of the Harris-Benedict data; do not conflate them.

Two traps: PubMed renders the 1991 equation as "370 ± 21.6 × FFM" — a **typesetting artifact**, the operator is `+`. And this equation is widely mislabelled "Katch-McArdle"; **Cunningham published it.** Validation: Thompson J, Manore MM (1996), J Am Diet Assoc 96(1):30-34, DOI 10.1016/S0002-8223(96)00010-7 — most accurate of four equations in 37 endurance athletes (within 158 kcal/d men, 103 kcal/d women).

**Rejected: Harris-Benedict (1918 original or the Roza & Shizgal 1984 revision).**
Harris JA, Benedict FG (1918), PNAS 4(12):370-373, DOI 10.1073/pnas.4.12.370 (verified — but this is the announcement note; the coefficients come from the 1919 Carnegie monograph). Roza AM, Shizgal HM (1984), Am J Clin Nutr 40(1):168-182, DOI 10.1093/ajcn/40.1.168 (verified): `Men RMR = 88.362 + 4.799·ht + 13.397·wt − 5.677·age`; `Women = 447.593 + 3.098·ht + 9.247·wt − 4.330·age`. **It lost** because Mifflin's own data showed it overestimating REE by 5% (p<0.01), Frankenfield 2013 placed it among the overestimating equations, and Roza's own precision is only ±14%.

**Setup must collect:** **sex** *(sex-specific — it changes the constant term: +5 vs −161)*, weight (kg), height (cm), age (y). Cunningham additionally needs FFM, i.e. a body-fat estimate (§5).

## 2. Activity multipliers

**Use the FAO bands.**
```
PAL = TEE / BMR
Sedentary or light activity ....... 1.40 – 1.69
Active or moderately active ....... 1.70 – 1.99
Vigorous or vigorously active ..... 2.00 – 2.40
```
FAO/WHO/UNU (2004). *Human Energy Requirements: Report of a Joint FAO/WHO/UNU Expert Consultation*, FAO Food and Nutrition Technical Report Series No. 1, Table 5.3 p.38. No DOI (UN technical report); **verified from the primary PDF and the HTML chapter, which agree verbatim.** The report notes "PAL values > 2.40 are difficult to maintain over a long period of time", and the same bands apply to men and women.

The NASEM alternative (DOI 10.17226/26818, verified) uses `inactive <1.53 / low active <1.68 / active <1.85 / very active <2.50`. **These cut-points are literally the 25th/50th/75th percentiles of its own doubly-labelled-water dataset** (n=4,299 adults: 10th/25th/50th/75th/90th = 1.39/1.53/1.68/1.85/2.03), so "inactive" means bottom quartile of measured adults, not a physiological threshold. NASEM 2023 also **abandoned BMR × PAL entirely**, fitting TEE directly from age, height and weight per activity category.

**Rejected: the ubiquitous gym ladder 1.2 / 1.375 / 1.55 / 1.725 / 1.9.** **No primary source exists.** A Europe PMC full-text search returns 13 papers, earliest 2007, every one a downstream user with no citation attached. The values match no published band set — none coincides with any FAO or NASEM boundary. They are an interpolated three-point scale: **1.375 = (1.2+1.55)/2 and 1.725 = (1.55+1.9)/2 exactly** (my arithmetic). Worse, the 1.2 anchor is physiologically wrong for a desk worker: 1.2 × BMR is Black et al.'s **non-ambulant** limit (bedridden, explicitly excluded from their free-living dataset; Eur J Clin Nutr 50(2):72-92, PMID 8641250 — **no DOI exists**; Crossref has no 1996 entries for that journal). FAO puts the free-living floor at **1.40**, so the gym scale systematically under-feeds sedentary users.

**Setup must collect:** self-reported activity category, ideally with occupation and step count as a cross-check. Not sex-specific.

## 3. Energy deficit and surplus sizing

**Fat loss: 0.5-1.0 %BW/week; 0.7% best supported.**
Garthe I, Raastad T, Refsnes PE, Koivisto A, Sundgot-Borgen J (2011). *Effect of Two Different Weight-Loss Rates on Body Composition and Strength and Power-Related Performance in Elite Athletes*. Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 21(2):97-104. DOI 10.1123/ijsnem.21.2.97 — **verified.** 0.7%/wk vs 1.4%/wk: lean body mass **+2.1±0.4%** in the slow group vs **−0.2±0.7%** in the fast (between-group p<0.01). Conclusion verbatim: athletes "should aim for a weekly BW loss of 0.7%".
Helms ER, Aragon AA, Fitschen PJ (2014). *Evidence-based recommendations for natural bodybuilding contest preparation: nutrition and supplementation*. J Int Soc Sports Nutr 11:20. DOI 10.1186/1550-2783-11-20 — **verified**, verbatim: "bodyweight losses of approximately 0.5 to 1%/wk to maximize muscle retention."

**Do not use 3,500 kcal per pound.** Hall KD, Sacks G, Chandramohan D, Chow CC, Wang YC, Gortmaker SL, Swinburn BA (2011). *Quantification of the effect of energy imbalance on bodyweight*. The Lancet 378(9793):826-837. DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(11)60812-X — **verified.** The static rule "ignores dynamic physiological adaptations to altered body weight". **Replacement, verbatim:** "every permanent change of energy intake of 100 kJ per day will lead to an eventual weight change of about 1 kg (equivalently, **10 kcal per day per pound** of weight change) and it will take about 1 year to achieve half of the total weight change". See also Hall KD, Chow CC (2013), Int J Obes 37(12):1614, DOI 10.1038/ijo.2013.112, and Thomas DM et al. (2013), Int J Obes 37(12):1611-1613, DOI 10.1038/ijo.2013.51 (both verified).

**Muscle gain: ~350-500 kcal/day surplus — weakly evidenced, and say so.**
Slater GJ, Dieter BP, Marsh DJ, Helms ER, Shaw G, Iraki J (2019). *Is an Energy Surplus Required to Maximize Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy Associated With Resistance Training*. Front Nutr 6:131. DOI 10.3389/fnut.2019.00131 — **verified**, and explicit that "the specific energy surplus required to facilitate muscle hypertrophy is **unknown**" and such estimates "have never been validated in a resistance training population". The one measured figure: Garthe I, Raastad T, Sundgot-Borgen J (2011), Appl Physiol Nutr Metab 36(4):547-554, DOI 10.1139/h11-051 (verified) — a counselled surplus of **506±84 kcal/d** with 4 strength sessions/wk gave BM +4.3±0.9%, LBM **+2.8±0.5%**, against ad-libitum controls at +1.0±0.6% BM and no LBM change. (Often miscited as "Garthe 2013"; it is 2011.)

**Rejected:** the 3,500 kcal/lb static rule (above), and large "bulk" surpluses — no validated sweet spot exists, and ~500 kcal/d already produced +2.8% LBM.

**Setup must collect:** current body weight (the rate is %BW-based), goal direction, training frequency. Not sex-specific.

## 5. Body fat from a tape measure

**US Navy circumference method.** Hodgdon JA, Beckett MB (1984), *Prediction of Percent Body Fat for U.S. Navy Men from Body Circumferences and Height*, NHRC Report 84-11, DOI **10.21236/ada143890** — **verified via Crossref**; and *...for U.S. Navy Women...*, NHRC Report 84-29, DOI **10.21236/ada146456** — **verified.**

**The equations predict density, not %BF** (metric, log base 10, circumferences in cm):
```
Men:   D = −0.19077·log10(abdomen II − neck) + 0.15456·log10(height) + 1.0324
Women: D = −0.35004·log10(abdomen I + hip − neck) + 0.22100·log10(height) + 1.29579
Then (Siri, printed verbatim in both reports):  %BF = 100 · [(4.95 / D) − 4.50]
```
**Accuracy vs hydrostatic weighing:** men n=602, R=0.90, SEE **3.52 %BF** (cross-validation n=100, SEM 2.70); women n=214, R=0.85, SEE **3.72 %BF** (cross-validations SEM 4.36 and 4.04).

Three traps that will silently corrupt an implementation:
1. **The metric and imperial forms are not algebraically equivalent.** The metric form returns density, which Siri makes a *nonlinear* function of the logs; the DoD imperial form returns %BF *linearly*. The imperial coefficients are a first-order linearisation, not a unit conversion. Agreement is ~±0.3 %BF normally, diverging to ~0.7 %BF at extremes. **Pick one and state which.**
2. **The abdomen site differs by sex** — not a naming variation. Men use **Abdomen II** (at the umbilicus). Women use **Abdomen I** (minimal width, midway xyphoid-umbilicus) **plus hip**.
3. Potter AW et al. (2022), Front Physiol 13:868627, DOI 10.3389/fphys.2022.868627 (verified) **prints the female equation omitting the hip term — a typo in the published Methods.** Do not copy it from there.

**Known bias**, from Potter 2022 (n=609, vs DXA): underestimates men by ~2.5 %BF, **overestimates women by 1.3-2.3 %BF**; overestimates lean subjects, underestimates high-%BF subjects. Its own verbatim conclusion: "AC %BF is not suitable for applications requiring quantitative body composition assessment." Merrill Z, Chambers A, Cham R (2020), Obes Sci Pract 6(2):189-195, DOI 10.1002/osp4.392 (verified) found RMSE 17.3% relative error in general working adults. **Use it for tracking change over time, never as an absolute number.**

**Rejected: Deurenberg BMI-based.** `%BF = 1.20·BMI + 0.23·age − 10.8·sex − 5.4` (R²=0.79, SEE 4.1 %BF). Deurenberg P, Weststrate JA, Seidell JC (1991), Br J Nutr 65(2):105-114, DOI 10.1079/bjn19910073 (verified). **It lost** because SEE 4.1 is worse than the Navy's 3.52/3.72, and BMI cannot separate muscle from fat — fatal for an app whose purpose is tracking FFM change under resistance training.
**Rejected: Jackson-Pollock skinfolds.** Jackson AS, Pollock ML (1978), Br J Nutr 40(3):497-504, DOI 10.1079/bjn19780152; Jackson AS, Pollock ML, Ward A (1980), Med Sci Sports Exerc 12(3):175-182, DOI 10.1249/00005768-198023000-00009 (both verified). Comparable accuracy, but **it lost on the stated constraint**: it needs calipers and a trained technician.

**Setup must collect:** **sex** *(sex-specific — selects the equation)*, height, neck, waist *(sex-specific site)*, **hip (women only)**, units consistent.

## 6. Weekly set volume — the counting rule

Pelland JC, Remmert JF, Robinson ZP, Hinson SR, Zourdos MC (2025). *The Resistance Training Dose Response: Meta-Regressions Exploring the Effects of Weekly Volume and Frequency on Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength Gains*. Sports Medicine 56(2):481-505. DOI 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w — **verified.** 67 studies, 2,058 participants. Posterior probability that the volume slope exceeds zero: **100% for both hypertrophy and strength**, with diminishing returns more pronounced for strength. It supplies the **fractional counting rule the engine should implement: a direct set counts 1.0, an indirect set 0.5.**

Currier BS, D'Souza AC, Fiatarone Singh MA, et al. (2026). *ACSM Position Stand. Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews*. Med Sci Sports Exerc 58(4):851-872. DOI 10.1249/mss.0000000000003897 — **verified; supersedes ACSM 2009 and is the current governing document.** 137 systematic reviews, >30,000 participants: hypertrophy enhanced by **≥10 sets/wk**; diminishing returns beyond **~18-20 weekly sets**.

**The curve is monotonic increasing and decelerating** — not a threshold, not an inverted U within the studied range. No meta-analysis identifies a volume at which hypertrophy declines. The honest statement is that *marginal* return approaches zero near 18-20 sets/muscle/week.

**Rejected: "10 sets/muscle/week is optimal".** The number 10 is a **category boundary that failed significance** in Schoenfeld 2017 (P=0.074). ACSM 2026's "≥10" is a **floor, not an optimum**.
**Rejected: Baz-Valle's 12-20 as the universal default.** Baz-Valle E et al. (2022), J Hum Kinet 81:199-210, DOI 10.2478/hukin-2022-0017 is sound (no difference moderate vs high for quadriceps p=0.19 or biceps p=0.59) but its population is **young trained men 18-35 only, 7 studies**. Currier 2023 (n>5,000, ~46% women) is the better generic anchor.

## 7. Choosing a split for 2-6 training days

**Frequency is a scheduling variable, not a growth variable.** Schoenfeld BJ, Grgic J, Krieger J (2019), J Sports Sci 37(11):1286-1295, DOI 10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906, verbatim: "there is **strong evidence that resistance training frequency does not significantly or meaningfully impact muscle hypertrophy when volume is equated**." The earlier Schoenfeld 2016 frequency result (ES 0.49 vs 0.30, P=0.002) is a **volume artifact**. For **strength**, frequency does matter: ACSM 2026 recommends **≥2 sessions/wk**, and Pelland's frequency slope has posterior probability 100% for strength but <100% for hypertrophy.

So the split's only job is to distribute the weekly set target across available days.

| Days | Split | Freq/muscle | Sets/muscle/wk | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Full body ×2 | 2× | **6-10** | Below the ≥10 floor for most muscles unless sessions run long. Still clears ACSM 2026's ≥2 sessions/wk for strength. State the constraint honestly rather than hiding it. |
| 3 | Full body ×3 *(prefer over PPL)* | 3× | **9-15** | PPL at 3 days gives only 1×/muscle/wk and forces all weekly volume into one session, where the last sets are least productive. |
| 4 | Upper / Lower ×2 | 2× | **12-16** | **Best evidence fit and the engine default** — matches Currier 2023's highest-ranked hypertrophy prescription (higher-load, multiset, twice-weekly, SMD 0.66, 95% CrI 0.47-0.85). |
| 5 | U/L/U/L + accessory or weak-point day | 2-3× | **14-18** | Approaching the 18-20 deceleration point. |
| 6 | Push/Pull/Legs ×2 | 2× | **16-20** | Top of the useful range; marginal return near zero beyond. |

Set counts use fractional counting (§6). Ranges reflect exercise selection, not split magic. **Not sex-specific** (Roberts 2020).

## 8. Fluid intake

**Baseline (sex-specific).** IOM (2005), *Dietary Reference Intakes for Water, Potassium, Sodium, Chloride, and Sulfate*, National Academies Press, DOI 10.17226/10925 — **verified**, verbatim: "The AI for total water intake for young men and women (ages 19 to 30 years) is **3.7 L and 2.7 L per day**, respectively", of which beverages supplied **3.0 L** and **2.2 L** — "approximately **81 percent** of total water intake", food ~19%.

> **Display the beverage figure — ~3.0 L men, ~2.2 L women — never "drink 3.7 L".** An app cannot measure the water in food, and the total AI includes it.

**During and after training — replace by mass, not by clock.** ACSM; Sawka MN, Burke LM, Eichner ER, Maughan RJ, Montain SJ, Stachenfeld NS (2007). *Exercise and Fluid Replacement*. Med Sci Sports Exerc 39(2):377-390. DOI 10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597 — **verified** (note: Crossref returns the short title and no author list; the full position-stand title and the six authors are confirmed via PubMed PMID 17277604). Verbatim: the goal is to prevent "excessive (**>2% body weight loss** from water deficit) dehydration", and "**customized fluid replacement programs are recommended**. Individual sweat rates can be estimated by measuring body weight before and after exercise."

Practical rule: weigh in and out; keep in-session loss **under 2% of body mass**; replace the deficit afterwards. A commonly cited replacement figure of **~1.5 L per kg lost** (150% of the deficit, when recovery is <12 h) is **[PARAPHRASE — not primary-verified**; the publisher returned HTTP 402]. **Obtain the PDF before that number ships.** This is additive to baseline, not part of it.

**Rejected: the "8×8" rule.** Valtin H (2002), *"Drink at least eight glasses of water a day." Really? Is there scientific evidence for "8 × 8"?*, Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol 283(5):R993-R1004, DOI 10.1152/ajpregu.00365.2002 — **verified**, verbatim: "**No scientific studies were found in support of 8 × 8.**" It is also sex-invariant where the DRI is not, and wrongly excludes caffeinated beverages.
**Rejected: a flat 3.5 L/day for everyone** (the app's current rule) — overshoots the female total AI by ~30%, undershoots the male, and ignores sweat-rate variability.

**Setup must collect:** **sex** *(sex-specific — 3.7 vs 2.7 L)*, body mass, and optionally pre/post-session weights.

## 9. Rest-interval defaults

| Exercise class | Default | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy multi-joint compound (≥80% 1RM, ≤6 reps) | **180-300 s** | de Salles 2009 (3-5 min at 50-90% 1RM); Grgic 2018 (>2 min, trained) |
| Moderate compound (6-12 reps) | **120-180 s** | Schoenfeld 2016 (3 min > 1 min at 8-12RM) |
| Single-joint isolation | **60-90 s** | Lower systemic cost |
| Machine / accessory | **60-90 s** | As above |

Schoenfeld BJ, Pope ZK, Benik FM, et al. (2016). *Longer Interset Rest Periods Enhance Muscle Strength and Hypertrophy in Resistance-Trained Men*. J Strength Cond Res 30(7):1805-1812. DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000001272 — **verified.** 21 trained men, 8 weeks, **1 min vs 3 min**: 3 min significantly better for 1RM squat, 1RM bench and anterior-thigh thickness.
Grgic J, Schoenfeld BJ, Skrepnik M, Davies TB, Mikulic P (2018). *Effects of Rest Interval Duration in Resistance Training on Measures of Muscular Strength: A Systematic Review*. Sports Med 48(1):137-151. DOI 10.1007/s40279-017-0788-x — **verified.** >2 min needed to maximise strength in trained individuals; 60-120 s sufficient in untrained.
de Salles BF, Simão R, Miranda F, Novaes J da S, Lemos A, Willardson JM (2009). *Rest Interval between Sets in Strength Training*. Sports Med 39(9):765-777. DOI **10.2165/11315230-000000000-00000** — **verified** (the commonly circulated `10.2165/00007256-200939090-00003` returns **404**).
Grgic J et al. (2017), Eur J Sport Sci 17(8):983-993, DOI **10.1080/17461391.2017.1340524** (the commonly circulated `...1372855` returns **404**).

**Contradicting evidence, stated rather than omitted:** ACSM 2026 found strength "was not affected by... short (<1 min) versus long (>1 min) between-set rest intervals" and "insufficient data" for hypertrophy. **Reconciliation:** the *acute* effect is uncontested — short rest reduces reps and load in subsequent sets — so rest acts on adaptation **through volume-load**, not as an independent stimulus. Prescribe long rest on heavy compounds because the cost is only clock time; do not claim rest duration is itself an adaptive variable. A null is not a reason to choose the short option.

> **Honest limit:** the literature stratifies rest by **load and goal**, not by exercise type. The multi-joint/single-joint mapping above is an engineering heuristic onto the load ranges actually tested — **INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE** for exercise-type stratification per se.

**Rejected: the 30-60 s "hypertrophy rest" default.** de Salles 2009 itself recommended it, reasoning from greater acute growth-hormone response. **It lost** because the direct longitudinal test (Schoenfeld 2016) found 1 min *worse* than 3 min, and because it substitutes an acute endocrine surrogate for a measured morphological outcome.

## 10. Double progression and the load increment

**The rule.** Fix the load; add reps within the prescribed range across sessions; when the top of the range is hit on all prescribed sets, increase the load and reset to the bottom.

**The citable anchor.** ACSM (2009), *Progression Models in Resistance Training for Healthy Adults*, Med Sci Sports Exerc 41(3):687-708, DOI 10.1249/mss.0b013e3181915670 (verified; PMID 19204579), verbatim: "When training at a specific RM load, it is recommended that **2-10% increase in load** be applied when the individual can perform the current workload for one to two repetitions over the desired number." ACSM 2026 does not cover load increments, so this stands unreplaced.

> **INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE:** no RCT tests "double progression" as a named method against an alternative. It is codified practice with an authoritative guideline behind it — present it as such, not as a validated finding.

**Effort target.** Zourdos MC et al. (2016), J Strength Cond Res 30(1):267-275, DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000001049 (verified): RPE 10 = 0 RIR, RPE 9 = 1 RIR, validated against mean concentric velocity (r=−0.88 experienced, −0.77 novice). Helms ER et al. (2016), Strength Cond J 38(4):42-49, DOI 10.1519/SSC.0000000000000218 (verified).

**Standard plate sizes (verified against manufacturer catalogues and federation rulebooks).**

| Modality | Smallest common step | Absolute floor |
|---|---|---|
| Metric barbell | **2.5 kg total** (pair of 1.25 kg) | 0.25 kg total (pair of 0.125 kg fractionals) |
| Imperial barbell | **5 lb total = 2.268 kg** (pair of 2.5 lb) | 0.5 lb = 0.227 kg (pair of 0.25 lb) |
| Dumbbells, metric | **5 kg per pair** (2.5 kg/bell) | 2 kg per pair at the light end |
| Dumbbells, imperial | **10 lb per pair** (5 lb/bell) | 5 lb per pair — steps are 2.5 lb/bell **below 30 lb** |
| Weight stack | **10 lb / 4.54 kg** per pin | 2.5 lb / 1.13 kg with an add-on |

Federation practice: **IPF** Technical Rulebook 2026 §6 — the bar "must always be a multiple of **2.5 kg**"; **IWF** TCRR 2020 §6.6.2 — "must always be a multiple of **one (1) kg**" (accessed via a national-federation mirror; `iwf.sport` returned 403).

**Recommended rule.**
```
Δ_target = p · L        p = 2.5%  upper-body compound and all isolation
                        p = 5.0%  lower-body compound
Δ = round_down_to_nearest_achievable_step(Δ_target, equipment)
Δ = max(Δ, Δ_min)
GUARD: if Δ_min / L > 0.10  →  DO NOT ADD LOAD; extend the rep range instead
```
The 2-10% band is genuinely citable (ACSM 2009). **The narrower 2.5%/5% split is INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE** — no study compares increment sizes head-to-head. It is a heuristic sitting inside a guideline range.

**The guard clause is the real engineering content.** For light isolation the equipment floor exceeds the guideline ceiling: a 20 kg curl in a metric gym faces Δ_min = 2.5 kg = **12.5% of working load**. Dumbbells are the worst case — the bilateral step (5 kg/pair) is **twice** the barbell step from an equivalent plate pair. The engine must extend the rep range rather than silently apply a 12.5% jump.

**Rejected: a fixed 2.5 kg / 5 lb increment for every lift** (the app's current rule). It violates the ACSM 2-10% band at **both ends simultaneously** — 1.7% of a 150 kg squat, 12.5% of a 20 kg curl.
**Rejected: velocity-based autoregulation.** Zourdos shows velocity is the *better* construct (it is the variable RIR was validated against). **It lost on instrumentation** — a linear position transducer or validated accelerometer per set, against RIR's zero hardware.

**Setup must collect:** unit preference (kg/lb), available plate denominations including whether micro-plates exist, dumbbell step, per-exercise equipment type. Not sex-specific.

## 11. Creatine dose

Kreider RB, Kalman DS, Antonio J, et al. (2017). *International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: safety and efficacy of creatine supplementation in exercise, sport, and medicine*. J Int Soc Sports Nutr 14:18. DOI 10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z — **verified.**
Antonio J, Candow DG, Forbes SC, et al. (2021). *Common questions and misconceptions about creatine supplementation: what does the scientific evidence really show?* J Int Soc Sports Nutr 18(1). DOI 10.1186/s12970-021-00412-w — **verified.**

- **Loading (optional):** "5 g of creatine monohydrate (or approximately **0.3 g/kg body weight**) four times daily for **5-7 days**."
- **Maintenance:** "**3-5 g/day**, although some studies indicate that **larger athletes may need to ingest as much as 5-10 g/day**." Antonio gives the body-mass form: "3-5 g or **0.1 g/kg** of body mass."
- **Loading is not required.** Antonio verbatim: "**you do not have to 'load' creatine.** Lower, daily dosages (i.e. 3-5 g/day) are effective", minimum 4 weeks. Kreider's alternative — 3 g/d for 28 days — states the trade-off: only a "gradual" rise in muscle creatine.
- **Engine rule:** maintenance `max(3 g, 0.1 g/kg)`, capped near 10 g/d. Optional load at 0.3 g/kg/d × 5-7 d.
- **Safety:** "up to 30 g/day for 5 years is safe and well-tolerated in healthy individuals."
- **Sex:** both sources discuss sex but **neither gives a sex-specific dose**. Kreider notes "some studies suggest that women may not see as much gain in strength and/or muscle mass"; Antonio reports potential as "a multifactorial therapeutic intervention across the lifespan in females". **Dose by body mass, not sex.** Antonio also refutes three common objections: no increase in total or free testosterone or DHT, no hair loss, and no kidney damage at recommended doses in healthy individuals.

**Rejected: creatine ethyl ester and buffered creatine (Kre-Alkalyn).** Kreider Position #5, verbatim: "**creatine monohydrate is the most extensively studied and clinically effective form**... in terms of muscle uptake and ability to increase high-intensity exercise capacity." They lose **on the exact outcome their marketing claims superiority on** — muscle creatine uptake — and switching forms also discards the entire safety database.

**Setup must collect:** body mass. Not sex-specific.
